Agreement-to-disagreement ratio¶

For each age cohort, Agree combines Strongly Agree and Agree, while Disagree combines Disagree and Strongly Disagree. The ratio is calculated as Agree ÷ Disagree. Neutral responses are excluded from the numerator and denominator.

  • A ratio above 1 means agreement outnumbers disagreement.
  • A ratio below 1 means disagreement outnumbers agreement.
  • A ratio equal to 1 means the two groups are the same size.
Agree n Disagree n Agree/disagree ratio Direction
Age cohort
18–21 125 175 0.71 More disagreement
22–25 156 145 1.08 More agreement
26–29 32 30 1.07 More agreement
30–35 7 6 1.17 More agreement

Interpretation¶

Among respondents aged 18–21, disagreement is more common than agreement: 46.5% disagree or strongly disagree, compared with 33.2% who agree or strongly agree. This produces an agreement-to-disagreement ratio of 0.71, meaning there are about 71 agreeing respondents for every 100 disagreeing respondents.

The ratios are slightly above 1 among respondents aged 22–25 (1.08) and 26–29 (1.07), indicating that agreement only narrowly outnumbers disagreement in these cohorts. The 30–35 cohort has the highest ratio (1.17), but this is based on only 14 respondents and should be interpreted cautiously. Neutral responses account for roughly one-fifth of the first three cohorts and are not included in the ratio.

Overall, the clearest pattern is greater disagreement among the youngest cohort; the older cohorts are much closer to an even balance. These are descriptive, unweighted differences and do not establish that age cohort is statistically associated with the attitude.

Agreement-to-disagreement ratio by education¶

The ratio again divides combined agreement by combined disagreement. Neutral responses are excluded from both parts of the ratio.

Agree n Disagree n Agree/disagree ratio Direction
Education level and year
Bachelors 1st year 58 58 1.00 Equal
Bachelors 2nd year 67 78 0.86 More disagreement

Masters 1st year 22 28 0.79 More disagreement

Interpretation¶

After combining bachelor's fourth- and fifth-year respondents into Bachelor's final year and excluding the single PhD respondent, there is still no simple progression across education levels. Bachelor's first-year respondents are evenly divided between agreement and disagreement, producing a ratio of 1.00. Disagreement outnumbers agreement in bachelor's second year (0.86) and is most pronounced among bachelor's third-year respondents (0.75).

The combined bachelor's final-year group is nearly balanced but leans slightly toward agreement (1.05): 82 respondents agree or strongly agree, compared with 78 who disagree or strongly disagree. Master's first-year respondents lean toward disagreement (0.79), while master's second-year respondents lean toward agreement (1.40). The master's second-year result should be interpreted cautiously because this subgroup contains only 29 respondents.

Overall, the differences across education groups are mixed rather than monotonic. The strongest disagreement appears among bachelor's third-year respondents, whereas master's second-year respondents show the strongest relative agreement. These results are descriptive and unweighted; the ratio excludes neutral responses and does not by itself establish a statistically significant association between education and attitudes toward politics.
